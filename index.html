image-based 3D reconstruction vs LiDAR

How close does photogrammetry get to a laser scan?

Road authorities are required to keep an inventory of street furniture, and a laser scanner is not always available. So: how accurately and completely can ordinary photographs recover this geometry, measured against a LiDAR scan of the same object?

Six objects were shot hand-held and reconstructed by four methods from two different families — the classical correspondence pipelines (COLMAP, hloc+COLMAP), which match features between views and triangulate, and the learned feed-forward models (MASt3R-GA, VGGT), which regress geometry directly from a trained prior. The two families fail in opposite ways, which is much of the point: when matching fails it leaves a hole, when a learned model fails it can invent plausible geometry that is simply wrong.

Every object is scored the same way — reconstruction thinned onto a 1 cm grid to match the reference's own sampling, then accuracy (how far its points sit from the reference), completeness (how much of the reference it covers) and their harmonic mean F1, at 3, 5 and 10 cm. Metric scale comes from a 9.5 cm marker in frame, not from the reference, so size is established independently of the thing being measured.

Objects
These six were not chosen to be representative — they were chosen to span two axes along which reconstruction is expected to break: surface (matte → semi-gloss → glossy → glazed) and geometry (size, elongation, self-similarity, self-occlusion). Each card notes what that object was picked to test. All belong to asset classes road authorities must inventory, so the findings apply to real practice rather than convenient lab objects.

Captured hand-held on an iPhone 13 Pro, full circle at close, middle and upper levels, from roughly 1.2× the object's height, on overcast days for diffuse light — only the shelter was shot under changing cloud. Each page shows all four reconstructions side by side in 3D with the full breakdown; sizes below are measured from the best-scoring reconstruction.
Bus shelter
The largest object, and the most self-occluding — glazed roof and side panels plus backlit advertising, so much of it is seen through or reflected off glass. The hardest of the six: no method passes 60% F1 at 3 cm.
Chosen to test transparent surfaces and extreme self-occlusion from inside viewpoints.
4.3 × 2.0 × 2.8 m · 96 photos · reference incomplete (far side unscanned)
Information sign
A printed panel behind a glossy cover that mirrors its surroundings — two near-identical flat faces, so its appearance shifts with viewpoint. All four methods still land within ~3 cm median, on the most incomplete reference of the set.
Chosen to test whether reflections block matching, and whether two similar faces get confused for one another.
2.6 m tall · 75 photos · reference incomplete (upper ⅔, one side)
Bench
The longest and lowest object — 4 m across but under a metre tall, so it is seen from above far more than from the side. Weathered timber on a steel frame: matte throughout, the least reflective surface here.
A control object: matte and well-behaved, no failure predicted.
4.1 × 2.2 × 0.8 m · 94 photos · reference incomplete (backrest, legs)
Bollard
The smallest object in the set — a compact painted-metal post with a domed cap, its semi-gloss facets changing brightness with viewpoint. Simple enough to still score highest of all six: 94% F1 at 3 cm.
Chosen to test whether specular facets defeat feature matching — otherwise a control.
26 × 26 cm × 1.0 m · 69 photos · reference incomplete (~90° sector)
Lamppost
The tallest and thinnest object — 6 m high but only ~15 cm across, and dark and almost textureless, so it offers little to match on. This is where VGGT breaks down: 22% F1 against COLMAP's 89%.
Chosen to test fine-structure recovery at working resolution, and visibility at grazing angles from a distance.
5.8 m tall · 105 photos · complete reference
Bus-stop sign
A semi-gloss printed flag on a slender pole — two very different scales in one object: a broad legible plate above a narrow column.
Chosen to test whether a symmetric pole gets duplicated by spurious matches.
3.5 m tall · 79 photos · complete reference
Tool
DBSCAN gap tuner

Four of the six LiDAR references are incomplete — parts of the object were never scanned. A reconstructed point sitting in one of those unscanned regions looks far from the reference, but that is missing reference, not a reconstruction error. Counting it as error would penalise exactly the methods that covered the object most fully.

The fix is to separate the two cases by shape: far points that form a coherent cluster mark a gap in the reference and are excluded, while isolated far points are genuine reconstruction noise and stay counted. DBSCAN does that clustering, and its three parameters decide where the line falls.

This tool makes that choice visible — move the sliders and watch accuracy, completeness and F1 respond, across every object, to see how much each conclusion depends on it. For the two objects whose reference is complete there are no gaps to exclude, so gap detection is off by default.
