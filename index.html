image-based 3D reconstruction vs LiDAR

How close does photogrammetry get to a laser scan?

Six pieces of ordinary street furniture were photographed by hand and reconstructed in 3D by four methods — two classical photogrammetry pipelines (COLMAP, hloc+COLMAP) and two learned feed-forward models (MASt3R-GA, VGGT). Each reconstruction is compared against a LiDAR scan of the same object, used as ground truth.

Every object is measured the same way: the reconstruction is thinned onto a 1 cm grid to match the reference's own sampling, and scored by accuracy (how far its points sit from the reference), completeness (how much of the reference it covers) and their harmonic mean, F1, at thresholds of 3, 5 and 10 cm.

Beyond the per-object comparison, three studies ask the practical questions: does it matter how you shoot, how many photos you need, and what the whole thing costs in time and memory.

Studies
Does the shooting style matter?
Field guidance: is it worth walking close to an object, or is a distant orbit enough? The same two objects shot three ways — close-range + distant, close-range only, distant only — with bootstrap confidence intervals to show which differences are real rather than noise.
2 objects × 3 approaches × 2 methods · 12 reconstructions
How many photos are enough?
Where the payoff for extra shooting stops. Nested subsets — each larger set contains the smaller one, so the only thing that changes is the count — reveal the point past which accuracy stops improving.
2 objects · N = 15…100 · up to 3 method variants
What does it cost to compute?
Whether a method is usable at scale: how wall-clock time, RAM and VRAM grow as photos are added, which pipeline stage dominates, and which resource runs out first. All runs on identical hardware.

Bus shelter
The largest object in the set — and the only enclosed structure, with a roof and side panels rather than a single surface. Also the hardest to reconstruct well: no method passes 60% F1 at 3 cm.
4.3 × 2.0 × 2.8 m · 96 photos · reference incomplete (far side unscanned)
Information sign
A flat sign panel on a slim post — mostly one large planar face, unlike everything else here. All four methods land within ~3 cm median, but its reference is the most incomplete of the set: only the upper two thirds of the pole, from one side.
2.6 m tall · 75 photos · reference incomplete (upper ⅔, one side)
Bench
The longest and lowest object — 4 m across but under a metre tall, so it is seen from above far more than from the side.
4.1 × 2.2 × 0.8 m · 94 photos · reference incomplete (backrest, legs)
Bollard
The smallest object in the set, and the simplest shape — a short round post. It scores highest of all six: 94% F1 at 3 cm.
26 × 26 cm × 1.0 m · 69 photos · reference incomplete (~90° sector)

The tallest and thinnest object — nearly 6 m high but only ~15 cm across, so it fills very little of each frame. This is where VGGT breaks down: 22% F1 against COLMAP's 89%.
5.8 m tall · 105 photos · complete reference
Bus-stop sign
A sign plate on a tall thin pole — two very different scales in one object: a broad flat plate and a narrow column beneath it.

Tool
DBSCAN gap tuner

Four of the six LiDAR references are incomplete — parts of the object were never scanned. A reconstructed point sitting in one of those unscanned regions looks far from the reference, but that is missing reference, not a reconstruction error. Counting it as error would penalise exactly the methods that covered the object most fully.

The fix is to separate the two cases by shape: far points that form a coherent cluster mark a gap in the reference and are excluded, while isolated far points are genuine reconstruction noise and stay counted. DBSCAN does that clustering, and its three parameters decide where the line falls.

This tool makes that choice visible — move the sliders and watch accuracy, completeness and F1 respond, across every object, to see how much each conclusion depends on it. For the two objects whose reference is complete there are no gaps to exclude, so gap detection is off by default.
